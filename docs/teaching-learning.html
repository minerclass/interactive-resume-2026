Instructional philosophy

Protect the thinking the assignment exists to produce.

Most of my career has been spent close to instruction. I taught high school social studies in Chicago Public Schools, mentored new teachers as a lead teacher, chaired a department in an alternative school, coached teachers, supported a K–5 building as assistant principal, and led curriculum and instructional technology across a district. That work keeps returning me to one practical question. What is this lesson, task, or tool actually asking students to think and do?

Generative AI has made that question urgent. A student can now turn in polished work without doing the reading, reasoning, or revising the assignment was designed to build. My research calls this unproductive success. The response I work toward is instructional design that keeps productive friction, the effort that builds understanding and judgment, while removing exclusionary friction, the barriers that punish students without teaching them anything.

For school and district leaders, this means curriculum, assessment, professional learning, and policy have to be decided together. Phone rules, device choices, and AI procurement shape what students practice as much as any curriculum guide does.

How the approach shows up

Six places the work becomes practice.

Curriculum design

Start from the thinking a unit is meant to build. Then decide where tools can scaffold, where they can take on routine work, and where students need to work unassisted.

Assessment

Look for evidence of learning in the process as well as the product, so that assessment rewards the reasoning a generated output cannot supply.

Professional learning

Build educator learning around teachers' own classroom decisions and disciplines rather than tool tutorials, and design it with the same care expected of student learning.

Social studies and civic learning

Balanced, inquiry-driven history and civics instruction, supported by the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks I developed for critical thinking and civic-minded reasoning.

Technology balance

Treat screens, devices, and AI features as instructional decisions. The useful question is what a screen asks students to practice, not only how long they use it.

Equity

Separate productive struggle from barriers tied to access, language, disability, or compliance burdens, so that rigor does not become exclusion.

Doctoral work

Curriculum, advocacy, and policy.

I am completing an Ed.D. in Curriculum, Advocacy, and Policy at National Louis University. My dissertation studies pedagogical friction in the age of generative AI. The proposal was defended in August 2026, the study received IRB approval in October 2026, and data collection is underway. Completion is expected in 2027.

The study uses a qualitative-dominant convergent mixed methods design grounded in constructivist qualitative inquiry. It asks how classroom-facing educators, building-level administrators, district/system-level leaders, and adult university students make sense of the ways generative AI changes effort, dialogue, authorship, judgment, and participation. It also asks what language, assessment expectations, instructional practices, professional supports, and leadership approaches help preserve learning-supportive friction while reducing unnecessary barriers. The study is in progress, and no findings are reported here.

Dissertation research page
Framework

Pedagogical friction

Noetic, rhetorical, existential, and infrastructural dimensions of educationally productive resistance, with a working distinction between productive and exclusionary friction.

Framework

Tertiary algorithmicity

An extension of Walter Ong's media ecology to generative AI, used to examine unproductive success, when polished output arrives without the learning it was meant to show.

Frameworks

C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T.

Social studies frameworks for balanced history and civic reasoning. C.O.R.E.: critical thinking, openness, respect, and engagement. H.E.A.R.T.: honesty, empathy, accountability, responsibility, and thoughtfulness.
